Common Signal

How this site is drawn: a civic network in X-Y vector geometry, a palette in which every colour has one job, and plates that move only as the visitor allows.

wireframe 0.4.0-alpha1 · samples verified 2026-11-19

A public machine

Common Signal is the editorial series this site belongs to. It draws a network as a city: roads, crossings, and destinations, laid out deliberately and lit where something means something. The conceit has one job, which is to make the mechanisms easier to see. Shared foundations, explicit rules, finite capacity, and the care that keeps a system working are civic virtues as much as engineering ones. What we connect, we should understand.

The city is a setting, not a product. wireframe is a Rust library; it does not run a city, a cloud, or a network operations centre. Where the analogy would mislead, it stops, and every technical figure uses the library’s own stages and types.

The palette of responsibility

Seven colours from the Common Signal mood book, each with one job. Void is the ground, cobalt draws the world, signal makes information legible, and flow marks the chosen path. Capacity, halt, and notice are kept for budgets, real stops, and proposals. None is a spare accent, and none carries meaning alone: every mark that matters also has a label, a line style, or a shape.

The contrast figures are for the flat colour as text. Cobalt falls short of 4.5:1 on the raised panel, so words in that hue use a lighter cut, #7a9ffa. The quiet border colour is decoration only; any edge that identifies a control is drawn in a stronger tick colour that holds 3:1. The logotype keeps its own green: it is the supplied artwork, and it is not recoloured to fit the palette.

The plates

Every illustration is a Three.js scene from leynos/wireframe-3js, served from this site at commit 57be19c, with the poster that source supplies for each. The site patches the diagrams so that each says what wireframe, or the proposal it shows, actually says, adds three of its own in the same hand, and renders a fresh still for every scene it redraws. Its renderer draws three-dimensional line segments in stroke order and lets light gather where they cross. Labels inside the art belong to the art; anything a reader needs is in the caption beside it.

Each caption says what kind of picture it is. A technical diagram follows the library’s real order of stages, simplified where it says so. Concept artwork illustrates an idea, often a proposal, and certifies nothing. A civic setting carries no technical claim. No plate is a measurement, and the moving packets are not traffic data.

Legal pages carry no plates.

How the plates move

With ordinary motion settings, a plate plays by itself: the hero as the page loads, the others as they come into view. A plate that scrolls out of view, or a tab left in the background, stops drawing until it returns.

If the browser asks for reduced motion, every plate starts as its complete still, with a Play illustration button. Pressing it plays that one plate and offers Show still; nothing else on the page moves. If the preference changes while a plate is playing, it stops at once. Under prefers-contrast: more the live plates draw without their halo. In the story, some scenes also follow the reader’s scroll: a camera moves in, the queued traffic slows to a stop while the queue is centred, and the glass bends under the strain. None of that happens under reduced motion. Without JavaScript or WebGL, and in print, the still is the plate.

Type and logotype

Sintony sets every word of interface and prose, at its two distributed weights. Monaspace Krypton sets code and byte sequences, with ligatures off so that ->, =>, and :: stay distinct. Both are SIL Open Font License faces, served from this site.

The logotype is the approved artwork, published as it was drawn rather than retyped or redrawn: lower-case letters built from beam lattices, beside a faceted rhombus. Its near-black ground is keyed to transparency, treating the art as light emitted over black, so its own glow carries it on every surface. The favicon is the rhombus alone. Common Signal is set in type beside it, as a series name, never as a second logo.
